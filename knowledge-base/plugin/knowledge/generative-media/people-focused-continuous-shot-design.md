# People-focused continuous-shot design

## Scope

This document explains how to conceive, research, and evaluate one continuous
moving-image shot centered on one or more people, independently of whether it
will be filmed, animated, or generated. It owns the shared decisions about the
shot's controlling development, canvas and duration, performance, blocking,
temporal composition, camera relationship, coordinated motion layers,
continuity, optional sound, and ending, while leaving medium-specific treatment
and prompt expression to their own responsibilities.

## When to update

Update this document when durable evidence from directing, cinematography,
animation, performance, motion analysis, or human-centered video generation
changes the decision model for staging people, attention, action, camera,
supporting movement, sound, or endings in one continuous shot.

## Judge quality against the shot's purpose

A good continuous shot is not defined by maximum movement, a complicated camera
path, or one pacing formula. It makes an intended person, relationship, action,
change, or emotion legible over time; directs attention deliberately; and uses
performance, space, camera, environment, and sound coherently.

Begin with the **controlling development**: the main visible or audible change
that the viewer should follow from the initial state to the end. It may be a
physical action, recognition, reaction, shift in relationship, reveal, change
of place or condition, or a deliberately sustained state. Backstory matters
only when it changes something the audience can perceive in this shot.

Establish the target duration, orientation, and aspect ratio before completing
the shot design. Also resolve intended use, crop or extension tolerance,
protected regions, viewing context, and looping when they affect performance or
composition. The source image or first composition does not silently decide the
final canvas.

Determine whether synchronized audio belongs to the artifact. A silent shot and
an audiovisual shot have different timing and performance responsibilities;
audio should not enter the plan accidentally or remain unspecified when it
controls the result.

## Read the initial state as a constraint

Identify the people, poses, gazes, contacts, props, setting, depth layers,
composition, camera position, light, and visible motion cues at the start.
Separate what is directly established from what the shot would have to invent
beyond the frame or behind an occluder.

Check whether the starting pose, balance, contact, crop, blur, directional
effect, malformed detail, or available space conflicts with the requested
development. The first visible motion should follow plausibly from the initial
state. When it cannot, change the shot or prepare a more suitable starting
image rather than silently substituting a convenient state.

An initial state is not a continuity freeze. Decide which properties must
remain stable, which may adapt, and which intentionally transform. A change in
expression, pose, relationship, wardrobe, environment, weather, light, or visual
state needs a beginning, development, and ending rather than an unexplained
jump.

## Design performance and blocking together

Translate intention into observable performance: direction of attention, gaze,
facial response, breath, posture, weight, gesture, action, reaction, and
recovery. Use the signals needed for the intended subtlety and shot scale.
Labels such as confident, shocked, tender, or exhausted do not replace visible
behavior.

Block each person relative to the camera, other people, interactive objects,
and the environment. Establish who initiates and receives each action, where
people begin and end, their paths and facing, changing distances, gaze targets,
contact points, and ownership of shared props.

For multiple people, treat the cast as an evolving relationship graph rather
than a list of parallel actions. Decide who controls attention in each phase,
how attention transfers, which reactions follow perception or contact, and how
overlap or crossing affects each person's silhouette and identity.

Preserve the visibility of information when it matters. A face, hand, shared
object, contact point, or reaction may be hidden deliberately, but accidental
occlusion should not erase the action the viewer needs to understand.

## Fit action and rhythm to duration

Use duration as an action budget. Select one controlling event or change and
add supporting beats only when they clarify its preparation, development,
consequence, atmosphere, or settling. Several attractive ideas do not become a
coherent shot merely because they fit in one sentence.

Distinguish ordered beats from simultaneous layers. A person's delayed reaction
may follow the main event; hair responding to a turn belongs to the same causal
phase. Allocate time according to readability and force rather than dividing
the duration evenly.

Speed includes more than a single adjective. Direction, magnitude,
acceleration, deceleration, pauses, impacts, holds, follow-through, and settling
can change the meaning and perceived weight of the same path. Stillness can
create tension, focus, contrast, or completion when it is chosen deliberately.

When the action budget is infeasible, identify which event controls. Extend the
duration, simplify the event, remove or subordinate competing actions, or
select another shot. Do not hide overload by accelerating every beat, combining
unrelated actions, or allowing the clip to end before its main change reads.

## Compose the frame through time

Treat the shot as a sequence of related compositions even when the camera is
fixed. Performance and object movement change balance, subject scale, negative
space, depth, eyelines, overlap, and visual hierarchy.

Decide what the audience should see first, where attention travels, and what
the ending composition leaves behind. Use placement, scale, overlap,
perspective, focus, value, color, edge, detail, directional motion, and
stillness as interacting controls rather than mandatory effects.

Organize foreground, middle ground, and background movement when they help
locate the people, expose space, create depth, or support the controlling
development. A continuous shot cannot use a cut to reset attention, so changes
in blocking, camera, focus, light, or activity must perform any necessary
reveal or transfer.

## Choose a motivated camera relationship

Always decide whether the camera is fixed or moving. The camera may observe,
accompany, discover, confront, follow, or subjectively identify with a person.
Its behavior should follow that relationship and the controlling development,
not a generic desire for cinematic motion.

For movement, define the path, direction, height, speed or energy, scale,
relationship to the subject, and stopping behavior when they affect the result.
Coordinate camera and person movement so the shot preserves required space,
visibility, and composition. A clear instruction is still a weak design when
the camera would cross impossible geometry, expose undesigned space, or compete
with the principal action.

Prefer the minimum camera behavior that realizes the purpose. A stable frame
can intensify performance; one simple movement can reveal or accompany it;
several combined moves earn their complexity only when the shot requires the
resulting change in viewpoint and attention.

## Coordinate the moving layers

Inspect every layer whose motion can affect the shot: people, facial expression
and gaze, interactive objects, hair and clothing, vehicles, liquids, particles,
foliage, weather, reflections, shadows, lighting, background planes, camera,
and sound when selected. Add other layers when the actual subject needs them.

Give supporting movement a cause. It may respond to body acceleration, force,
gravity, wind, contact, environment, camera perspective, light, or another
observable event. Dependent motion can lead, lag, overlap, overshoot, oscillate,
or settle, but should not start and stop independently without an intended
reason.

Choose richness deliberately. Environmental action, effects, light changes,
and layered motion can add atmosphere, scale, depth, and consequence. Remove
them when restraint, stillness, isolation, or graphic simplicity is the desired
character. Sparse and highly active shots are both valid only when their motion
density is designed rather than left to default.

## Define continuity and the end state

Identify the identities, designs, physical relationships, objects, setting,
visual medium, light, and spatial facts that must remain coherent. Protect only
the invariants the shot needs; continuity that freezes every property can
conflict with the intended action or transformation.

Settle what the people, camera, environment, and applicable sound are doing at
the duration boundary. The shot may arrive at a completed pose or relationship,
hold on a reaction, let supporting motion settle, continue beyond the frame,
or return near its initial state. Running out of time is not an ending design.

For a loop, design compatibility in pose, expression, contacts, framing, light,
moving layers, and motion direction or velocity at the join. Do not promise a
seamless loop when the controlling event is irreversible or the starting state
cannot recur naturally.

When generated audio is selected, establish each important sound's source,
timing, spatial or off-screen relationship, and synchronization. Exact dialogue
needs a speaker, wording, language, delivery, and feasible duration. Music and
ambience should support the shot's rhythm and point of view rather than becoming
an unrelated inventory.

## Research what the shot cannot safely invent

Separate user-owned creative choices from facts and observable evidence that
can be investigated. Ask the user about meaning, preference, priority,
permission, and material alternatives; research unfamiliar reality or visual
language instead of making the user reconstruct it.

Research is warranted when a material decision depends on unfamiliar or exact:

- period, place, culture, ceremony, gesture, relationship, or symbolism;
- occupation, uniform, tool, vehicle, weapon, sport, dance, martial art, craft,
  or medical action;
- body mechanics, contact, object transfer, force, resistance, or reaction;
- architecture, terrain, crowd, reflective surface, or another condition that
  constrains blocking or camera movement;
- garment, hair, prop, fluid, weather, particle, or environmental response;
- photographic, animation, performance, camera, or compositing convention; or
- the realistic duration, phase order, or loop behavior of a recognizable
  action.

Prefer direct observation, creator-owned visual material, professional and
scientific authorities, and original technical sources. Convert findings into
the few visible performance, timing, spatial, camera, or material decisions the
shot needs. A reference video may inform the design without becoming an input
to the final production system.

## Plan the necessary visual evidence

Identify which unresolved questions need visual material rather than more
verbal description. Useful roles include identity, costume or construction,
initial pose, contact, movement phases, camera path, location, lighting,
material response, animation cadence, and broader visual language.

For each material, define the evidence it must contain, which part of the design
it informs, and which unrelated properties should not transfer. Separate
material used to understand the shot from material that must be supplied to a
production or generation system.

When a hard requirement depends on missing evidence, expose the consequence.
Exact identity, unfamiliar interaction, specialized movement, or a recognizable
style may require the user to supply material, relax the requirement, or accept
an approximation. Missing optional material should not block a coherent shot;
state what it could improve and continue with supported choices.

## Critique the complete shot

Review the whole progression before treating it as ready. Check whether it
serves the intended use, establishes one controlling development, makes people
and relationships legible, fits the duration, composes attention through time,
motivates its camera, coordinates its moving layers, preserves the necessary
continuity, and arrives at a deliberate ending.

Revise a design that substitutes labels for observable performance, gives every
layer equal emphasis, overloads its action budget, hides required information,
adds camera motion without purpose, invents incompatible space, or ends in the
middle of its main change. A minimal shot can pass when its stillness, fixed
camera, quiet environment, and limited detail are intentional and effective.

The shot is ready when its canvas and duration are explicit; its initial state,
controlling development, performance, blocking, camera, layers, optional sound,
and end state form one feasible progression; every material person, action,
interaction, and constraint has an owner; and remaining gaps are low impact,
explicitly delegated, or disclosed.
