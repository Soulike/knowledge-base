# People-focused photographic-image design

## Scope

This document explains how to conceive, research, and evaluate a people-focused
still image that should read as a photograph or physically plausible
photorealistic capture. It owns medium-specific decisions about photographic
genre, direction, viewpoint, framing, perspective, focus, lighting, exposure,
motion, pose, and material response, while treating camera metadata as a means
to visible effects rather than a quality incantation.

## When to update

Update this document when durable evidence from photographic practice, optics,
portraiture, lighting, or physically based appearance changes the decision
model for people-focused photographic imagery or exposes a missing realism or
research responsibility.

## Choose the photographic situation

Decide what kind of photograph the image should appear to be before selecting
camera language. A formal studio portrait, environmental portrait, fashion or
editorial image, documentary observation, event photograph, publicity still,
street photograph, family snapshot, and paparazzi-like frame imply different
relationships among subject awareness, pose, lighting, composition, polish,
and environmental context.

Resolve the degree of direction. A deliberately posed subject can still look
natural when weight, hands, gaze, clothing, and expression are coherent. A
candid image needs visible evidence of divided attention, ongoing interaction,
mid-transition gesture, imperfect asymmetry, event-driven framing, or available
light; the word "candid" alone does not create those conditions.

For multiple people, decide whether the photograph is a formal group, an
observed interaction, or an environmental scene. Resolve relative height,
spacing, depth, overlap, gaze, contact, and shared focus. A shallow focus plane
or tight crop that flatters one portrait can make a group unreadable.

## Describe viewpoint before equipment

Perspective follows the camera or viewer position relative to the people and
environment. Establish camera height, angle, subject distance, lateral
position, and whether the viewer feels intimate, observational, distant,
overhead, or low to the ground. These choices determine which surfaces are
visible, how near and far forms relate, and whether facial or body proportions
appear compressed or exaggerated.

Focal length determines field of view and magnification for a fixed camera
position. The familiar wide-angle and telephoto perspective effects usually
also involve changing camera distance to preserve subject size. Describe the
observable result—close environmental perspective, neutral portrait
perspective, compressed background, wide context, or intimate facial crop—then
add an approximate lens class only when it communicates an understood effect.

Avoid arbitrary equipment lists. A camera brand, lens model, film stock, or
millimeter value is useful when the user cares about that process or its visible
signature; it does not substitute for viewpoint, framing, focus, exposure,
color, and material decisions.

## Resolve framing and focus

Choose orientation, aspect ratio, shot distance, camera angle, headroom, look
room, crop points, foreground context, and background coverage according to the
image's subject. Tight framing can prioritize face and expression; wider
framing can make body language, relationship, occupation, or location part of
the portrait.

State what must be in focus. For one person, this may be the nearer eye, the
whole face, the full body, or a significant object. For a group, determine
whether every important face must share an acceptable focus plane. Depth of
field depends on aperture, focus distance, focal length, format, and
subject-background separation, so extreme blur is not automatically desirable.
Preserving environmental context may require more depth rather than less.

Use foreground and background blur, sharpness, contrast, and detail to support
hierarchy. Do not combine a demand for a fully legible environment with an
indiscriminately erased background.

## Design motivated light and exposure

Identify the sources that plausibly illuminate the scene: daylight, window,
sky, flash, softbox, lamp, sign, fire, reflected light, or another practical
source. Resolve direction, apparent size or hardness, color, intensity,
distance, falloff, and the balance between subject and environment.

Light direction changes facial structure, volume, and mood. Soft light creates
gradual transitions; hard light creates sharper shadow boundaries and stronger
contrast. Front, side, back, overhead, and under-lighting produce different
visible form and should be chosen for the intended result rather than treated
as interchangeable mood labels.

Keep highlights, cast shadows, contact shadows, reflections, and background
illumination compatible with the same sources. Decide whether exposure protects
faces, highlights, shadow detail, silhouettes, bright practical lights, or an
intentional over- or underexposed effect. Photographic plausibility does not
require neutral exposure, but it does require an intelligible cause for the
chosen appearance.

## Make pose, motion, and materials physically coherent

Specify weight-bearing support, shoulder and hip orientation, hands, gaze,
facial tension, and contact with people, props, furniture, or ground. Replace
"natural pose" with visible behavior appropriate to the photographic
situation. For interactions, ensure body distance, direction, and contact can
coexist from the selected viewpoint.

Choose whether motion is frozen, carried by a sharp body with secondary fabric
or hair movement, recorded as subject blur, or accompanied by camera motion.
Shutter terminology should follow that visible decision rather than appear as
decorative technical metadata.

Realism depends on materials responding differently but consistently to the
same light. Skin combines color variation, fine texture, gloss, soft shading,
and subsurface appearance; hair forms volumes and strands with directional
highlights; cloth varies by weave, roughness, thickness, fold, tension, and
sheen. Metal, glass, leather, water, and painted surfaces likewise need
material-specific reflections and edges. Maximum pore, weave, and sharpness
detail everywhere often weakens focus and photographic credibility.

Permit the imperfections appropriate to the genre: transient expression,
slight asymmetry, partial occlusion, motion, flare, grain, imperfect styling,
or uneven available light. Do not add defects mechanically; use them only when
they explain how the photograph was captured and support its intended
character.

## Research photographic and real-world claims

Research when the requested image depends on an unfamiliar optical effect,
camera or lighting process, historical photographic process, film stock,
specialist equipment, real location, period or cultural setting, exact clothing
or props, documentary conditions, or demanding body mechanics. Prefer optics
and camera sources for physical capture behavior, primary records for the
depicted subject, and direct examples from the requested photographic genre.

When researching a reference photograph, separate its subject matter from its
photographic choices. Analyze viewpoint, distance, crop, focus plane, motion,
source direction and hardness, exposure, color, posing, staging, material
response, and environmental context. Reproduce only the properties that serve
the new image.

The medium design is ready when the genre, degree of direction, viewpoint,
framing, focus, lighting, exposure, motion, pose, and material behavior describe
one plausible photographic event and the requested technical terms correspond
to visible effects rather than unsupported metadata.
