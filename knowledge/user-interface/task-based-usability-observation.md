# Task-based usability observation

## Scope

This document explains how observing someone attempt a realistic interface task can reveal usability problems and how to interpret a small set of observations. It covers the choice of task and participant context, neutral observation, and the evidence needed to discuss a problem without treating a participant's preference or a successful finish as a complete account of the experience.

## When to update

Update when evidence changes the principles for selecting realistic tasks, observing uncoached use, interpreting small qualitative samples, or prioritizing observed usability problems.

## Replace preference debates with a task

People on a design team can disagree about what an imagined average user would prefer. A more useful question is whether someone with a particular goal and relevant background can use the current interface to achieve it. Define that goal from actual user needs, product requirements, or other use evidence. If those sources are unavailable, label a proposed task as a hypothesis rather than presenting it as representative.

Provide the context a participant needs, such as domain knowledge, device, and account access, without supplying the intended route through the interface. A specialist task calls for someone who knows the domain; a novice task calls for a novice context. The observed outcome belongs to that task and context, not to an abstract average user.

## Observe the route as well as the result

Watch a participant attempt the task without coaching the next action. Record what they first try, where they hesitate or guess, what they expect an action to do, what actually happens, and whether they recognize and recover from a wrong turn. A completed task can still expose uncertainty, avoidable effort, or a costly workaround. A transient wrong turn that is quickly recognized and harmless may matter less than an error with consequential or difficult recovery.

Keep observed behavior distinct from the participant's explanation and from the observer's proposed cause. Questions after the attempt can clarify an observation; leading questions during it can change the route being observed. Start with an early design or prototype when it supports the question, then repeat after meaningful changes. A partially interactive artifact can support only the tasks and outcomes it actually exposes.

## Interpret a small study as discovery

A few sessions can reveal concrete problems in the tested tasks. They do not measure how often all users encounter a problem or prove that unobserved paths are clear. Report the task, participant context, observed action and result, and uncertainty behind each finding. Rank findings by the blocked goal, consequence of an error, recovery cost, and clarity of evidence; use repetition within the sessions as context, not a population rate. Decisions about a remedy follow from the observed problem and product constraints, and should be checked in a later observation.
