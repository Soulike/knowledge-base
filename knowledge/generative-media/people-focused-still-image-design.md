# People-focused still-image design

## Scope

This document explains how to conceive, research, and evaluate a still image in
which one or more people are the visual focus, independent of whether the image
will be photographed, illustrated, or generated. It owns the shared decisions
about the visible proposition, cast, relationships, action instant, stage
geometry, environment, visual hierarchy, and research readiness, while leaving
medium-specific rendering and prompt expression to their own responsibilities.

## When to update

Update this document when durable evidence from visual-development practice,
composition analysis, portraiture, or human-centered image generation changes
the decision model for staging people, relationships, actions, environments, or
visual hierarchy in one still frame.

## Judge quality against the image's purpose

A good people-focused image is not defined by one composition formula or a
maximum amount of detail. It makes the intended person, relationship, action,
or emotion legible; organizes the frame so that the viewer encounters that idea
deliberately; and uses the chosen setting and medium coherently. Symmetry and
asymmetry, centered and off-center placement, tight and environmental framing,
or stillness and forceful motion can all succeed when they serve the intended
reading.

Begin with the **visible proposition**: the main fact or feeling that the viewer
should understand from the image itself. It may be a person's identity, an
expression, a physical action, a relationship, or a contrast between a person
and the surrounding world. Treat backstory as useful only when it changes
something visible in the selected frame.

Always establish the target orientation and explicit aspect ratio before
composing the frame. Also resolve intended use, cropping tolerance, viewing
size, and required negative space when they affect the composition. A profile
image, book cover, poster, and cinematic landscape frame can require different
figure scale and empty-space decisions even when they depict the same subject.
When a reference image has a different ratio or crop, decide whether its
composition should control the new frame or adapt its relevant content to the
target canvas; do not inherit the reference canvas accidentally.

## Build a legible cast and relationship graph

Give every visually distinct person enough stable characteristics to remain
recognizable within the frame. Choose only attributes that affect this image,
such as silhouette, apparent age, body proportions, face, hair, wardrobe,
accessories, expression, or visual prominence. Small descriptive details cannot
rescue people whose large shapes and roles are indistinguishable.

For multiple people, treat the group as a relationship graph rather than a list
of characters. Establish:

- which person or relationship leads the visual hierarchy;
- who performs and receives each action;
- each person's left-right and foreground-background position;
- body orientation, gaze, distance, and relative height;
- the exact contact point for touch, shared props, or physical support; and
- enough separation or overlap to make the intended grouping readable.

Gaze, gesture, orientation, proximity, and contact can communicate a
relationship without explanatory text. Make those signals agree. Two people
described as intimate but staged with unrelated gazes, closed body orientation,
and no spatial connection do not visibly express the premise.

When several figures overlap, inspect silhouettes and negative spaces around
heads, hands, arms, and shared objects. Deliberate overlap can create depth and
connection; accidental tangencies or merged contours can obscure anatomy,
ownership, and action.

## Select one readable instant

A still image can imply a larger event but can display only one visible state.
Choose the phase of the action whose pose and physical consequences communicate
the event most clearly. Evaluate that instant through:

- the line of action and overall silhouette;
- balance, weight-bearing support, and center of gravity;
- limb placement, joint range, and body orientation;
- facial tension, gaze, and hand shape;
- contact with people, props, ground, or furniture; and
- secondary response in hair, fabric, water, dust, or nearby objects.

Translate a narrative such as "she has just escaped through the rain" into
current evidence such as posture, breath, wet materials, direction of gaze,
surface reflections, and environmental disturbance. Do not ask the image to
show a sequence of beginning, transition, and outcome simultaneously.

Research or obtain a pose reference when unfamiliar anatomy, force, contact,
foreshortening, sport, dance, craft, combat, or constrained clothing makes the
body mechanics uncertain. A dramatic pose remains weak when its balance or
interaction is physically unintelligible.

## Stage the frame

Choose the viewpoint and frame as part of the meaning, not after the people are
designed. Resolve camera or viewer height, angle, distance, crop, figure scale,
and the amount of environment that must remain visible. A closer frame can
prioritize expression; a wider frame can make place, movement, or social
distance part of the subject.

Organize foreground, middle ground, and background only when they help locate
the people and action. Use placement, scale, overlap, perspective, focus, value,
color, edge sharpness, detail density, and directional lines to establish depth
and reading order. These are interacting controls rather than a checklist: one
strong separation mechanism may make several others unnecessary.

Treat composition devices such as centered balance, thirds, diagonals,
triangles, frames within the frame, or leading lines as optional solutions to a
specific hierarchy problem. Do not select one before knowing what the viewer
should notice first and where the eye should travel next.

## Make the environment earn its place

The setting should support at least one concrete responsibility: locate the
event, make the action possible, reveal character or relationship, establish
scale or depth, motivate light, or reinforce the intended mood. Props should
likewise participate in identity, action, or setting rather than form an
unprioritized inventory.

Keep environmental perspective, contact, light direction, shadows, and scale
compatible with the people. Control background contrast, saturation, edges, and
detail so that the environment supports the visible proposition without
accidentally becoming a competing subject.

Light and color should make important forms readable and establish an intended
hierarchy. Decide where the main contrast belongs, which surfaces catch or lose
light, how figures separate from the environment, and whether the palette
unifies or deliberately contrasts the people and setting.

## Research what the frame cannot safely invent

Separate user-owned creative decisions from facts that can be investigated.
Ask the user about meaning, preference, priorities, and material alternatives;
research factual or visual evidence instead of asking the user to reconstruct
it.

Research is warranted when a visible decision depends on unfamiliar or exact:

- period, place, culture, religion, ceremony, social practice, or symbolism;
- occupation, uniform, tool, vehicle, weapon, sport, dance, or craft;
- clothing construction, architecture, furniture, landscape, weather, or
  material behavior;
- real-person or recurring-character identity;
- body mechanics, interaction, contact, or strong foreshortening;
- visual tradition, genre, production form, artwork, or creator; or
- text, insignia, gesture, or object whose meaning affects the image.

Prefer sources that own the fact being represented: archives, museum
collections, official maps and manuals, subject-matter institutions, direct
observation, and creator-owned model sheets or portfolios. Use several visual
references when one image would entangle identity, pose, costume, composition,
and style that should be decided independently.

The design is ready when its canvas and aspect ratio are explicit; it can be
described as one coherent visible frame; every important person, action,
relationship, and constraint has an owner and position; its medium, atmosphere,
setting, hierarchy, and intended detail density serve the same proposition; and
remaining gaps are low impact or explicitly delegated.
