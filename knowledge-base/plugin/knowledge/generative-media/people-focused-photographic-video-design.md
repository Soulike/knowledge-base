# People-focused photographic-video design

## Scope

This document explains how to research and evaluate the photographic treatment
of a continuous people-focused shot that should read as live-action or
physically plausible photorealistic footage. It owns medium-specific decisions
about directed versus observed behavior, body and contact mechanics,
photographic perspective and camera character, focus, exposure, motion
rendering, light, and material response through time.

## When to update

Update this document when durable evidence from cinematography, optics,
biomechanics, lighting, physical simulation, or photorealistic moving-image
practice changes the treatment of human motion, camera perception, focus,
exposure, light, or materials within this medium.

## Establish the photographic situation

Decide what kind of recorded moment the shot should appear to be. A directed
dramatic performance, formal portrait in motion, fashion film, documentary
observation, event recording, publicity shot, street encounter, family video,
and surveillance-like view imply different relationships among performer
awareness, camera access, polish, light, and environmental activity.

Resolve the degree of direction. A rehearsed performer can remain natural when
weight, gaze, hands, breath, expression, and timing form one behavior. An
observational shot needs visible evidence of attention elsewhere, ongoing
activity, imperfect timing, reactive framing, or available conditions; calling
the result candid does not create those properties.

Choose the camera's photographic character in relation to the established shot.
A stable, floating, shoulder-mounted, reactive, or rough handheld view implies
a different observer and energy. Describe that visible relationship rather than
using cinematic as an undifferentiated quality label.

## Make body mechanics and contact credible

Large voluntary actions usually involve preparation, changes in support and
balance, acceleration, deceleration, and recovery. Coordinate the center of
mass, support foot, torso counteraction, limb path, and momentum according to
the action's force and scale. Small eye or finger movement should not provoke
the same whole-body response as a jump, impact, or sudden turn.

Break a complex object interaction into meaningful phases when needed:
approach, preshape, contact, secure grasp or load transfer, manipulation,
release, and withdrawal. A hand cannot move an object securely before contact;
one person cannot release a shared load before the receiver supports it.

For person-to-person contact, settle the initiator, receiver, contact point,
direction and intensity, receiver response, duration, separation, and ending
support state when they affect the result. Review sliding feet, unsupported
weight, interpenetration, snapping joints, missing reaction, and objects that
detach or change ownership without a physical transition.

Physical plausibility is not the same as literal realism. Stylized speed,
camera motion, or exaggerated force can succeed in photographic imagery when
the shot establishes that intention and keeps its internal causes and effects
coherent.

## Preserve perspective through camera and subject motion

Read the initial image for apparent camera height, angle, distance, field of
view, focus plane, and available movement space. Do not infer an exact lens,
sensor, aperture, or rig from appearance alone.

Distinguish camera behaviors by their visible effect. Rotation redirects
attention from one position. Camera translation changes parallax and the
spatial relationship among foreground, subject, and background. Optical change
alters field of view and magnification. Describe the resulting perspective,
stability, and energy rather than naming equipment that does not settle them.

Coordinate apparent camera displacement with the established space. Check
whether motion would change perspective coherently, reveal unsupported
geometry, collide with a visible obstacle, or require a camera position that
the initial view cannot plausibly reach.

Use focal-length language only when it communicates an understood perceptual
effect such as an expansive environmental view, neutral portrait rendering,
compressed depth, or an intimate facial crop. Arbitrary equipment models and
millimeter values do not replace camera position, subject distance, framing,
and perspective.

## Direct focus, exposure, light, and motion rendering

Treat focus and depth of field as attention and staging decisions. Define which
person, eye, object, or depth plane should remain sharp as people move. Focus
may follow one subject, transfer attention, hold in anticipation, or permit
deliberate softness. Extreme shallow focus can make a moving group or
interaction unreadable.

Identify plausible light sources and how their direction, hardness, color,
falloff, and exposure relationship change as people or camera move. Faces, cast
shadows, contact shadows, highlights, reflections, and practical lights should
respond to the same geometry. A lighting change needs a cause such as movement
past a source, occlusion, weather, flicker, or an explicit event.

Decide which exposure information should remain visible through the shot:
faces, bright practical sources, highlight texture, shadow detail, silhouettes,
or an intentional over- or underexposed effect. Photographic plausibility does
not require neutral exposure, but it does require an intelligible reason for
how important forms appear or disappear.

Specify motion-rendering character only when it matters: natural motion blur,
crisp or staccato movement, smeared dreamlike movement, slow motion, or time
lapse. Frame rate and shutter terminology may help when the user cares about a
particular process, but the design responsibility is the visible temporal
effect and the preservation of important poses and contacts.

## Coordinate physical materials and the environment

Derive supporting motion from causes such as body acceleration, inertia,
gravity, wind, contact, collision, friction, or active handling. Hair, fabric,
jewelry, props, liquids, particles, foliage, and smoke should not begin and end
as one generic movement layer.

Different materials respond with different weight, stiffness, drag, elasticity,
collision, oscillation, and settling. Loose cloth can lag behind a turn; heavy
fabric resists it; rigid accessories preserve shape; hair volumes and strands
respond on different scales. Protect body and self-collision, attachment, and
contact rather than adding secondary motion that penetrates or detaches.

Coordinate wind and other environmental forces across the scene. Reflections,
highlights, shadows, dust, water, and vegetation should agree with the same
motion and light causes. Supporting response can enrich depth and atmosphere,
but it should not make every layer independently active.

## Research photographic and physical claims

Research when a recognizable result depends on unfamiliar or exact:

- dance, sport, martial arts, medical action, specialized locomotion, tool use,
  or other body mechanics;
- multi-person contact, object transfer, load bearing, collision, force, or
  reaction;
- a named camera movement, format, lens treatment, focus transition, shutter
  effect, or practical-light environment;
- architecture, terrain, vehicles, mirrors, water, or another physical
  condition that constrains the apparent camera or material response; or
- garments, hair, flexible props, fluids, weather, particles, and other
  visually dominant materials.

Prefer direct visual evidence, professional or scientific authorities, and
original technical sources. Convert findings into the few observable
mechanical, perspective, focus, light, or material constraints that affect the
shot. Do not turn equipment manuals or numerical settings into universal prompt
syntax.

## Critique the photographic treatment

Review whether directed or observed behavior fits the intended photographic
situation; bodies, contacts, and transferred forces remain credible;
perspective changes agree with camera and subject motion; focus and exposure
direct attention; and light, reflections, motion blur, hair, clothing, props,
and environment respond to intelligible causes.

Revise treatment that substitutes camera names for visible behavior, infers
false numeric precision from the initial image, changes perspective or focus
without cause, introduces physically unsupported contact, or makes every
material share the same motion. Deliberate handheld instability, blur,
stylization, or physical exaggeration can pass when it remains controlled and
serves the shot.

The photographic treatment is ready when its camera perception, human and
material mechanics, focus, exposure, light, and motion rendering form one
physically intelligible or deliberately stylized system throughout the shot.
