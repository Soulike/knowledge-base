# People-focused anime illustration design

## Scope

This document explains how to choose, research, and evaluate the visual language
of a people-focused anime- or manga-influenced still illustration. It owns
medium-specific decisions about proportion and abstraction, shape language,
line, value and color, rendering, effects, character-background integration,
and anime-specific research, without treating "anime" as one style or requiring
one production technique.

## When to update

Update this document when durable evidence from anime production, manga and
illustration practice, character design, or stylized rendering changes the
decision model for people-focused anime illustration or reveals a missing
medium-specific research responsibility.

## Specify the intended illustration form

"Anime" does not determine a complete visual result. First identify the kind of
artifact and viewing experience the image should resemble: a polished key
visual, animation-like frame, light-novel illustration, manga panel, character
sheet, game portrait, chibi image, graphic poster, painterly illustration, or
another form. This choice changes proportion, line, value grouping, rendering
density, background treatment, and effects.

Determine which impression controls the illustration before selecting details.
A quiet character portrait, comedic reaction, action key visual, and ensemble
scene need different exaggeration, pose readability, composition, and
figure-background relationships even when they share the same characters.

## Design from large decisions to small ones

### Proportion, abstraction, and silhouette

Choose a head-to-body regime, facial abstraction, eye and feature treatment,
hand and foot simplification, and degree of anatomical exaggeration that fit
the intended form. Do not apply one head ratio as a universal anime rule.
Relative height, shoulder width, limb length, head size, and facial placement
should distinguish characters while remaining coherent within the same image.

Use shape language and large-to-small contrast to communicate role and keep
characters recognizable before costume detail is visible. Test the outer
silhouette, internal negative spaces, and overlap between people. Strong hair,
clothing, accessory, and body masses should cooperate rather than compete as
unrelated identifiers.

### Pose and expression

Build pose and expression around the selected visible instant. Establish line
of action, balance, weight shift, torso and pelvis orientation, gaze, hands, and
contact before adding hair or fabric motion. Stylization may exaggerate these
signals, but it cannot replace a readable action or relationship.

Coordinate brows, eyes, mouth, head angle, shoulders, hands, and posture so they
express the same emotional state. Decide whether the effect is subtle,
graphic, comedic, or physically intense rather than requesting an abstract
emotion with no visible carrier.

### Line system

Treat line as a system. Decide whether contours are present, open or closed;
whether their edges are clean, rough, soft, or textured; how weight changes
between focal and secondary forms; whether interior lines are lighter than
outer contours; and how line color relates to skin, hair, clothing, light, and
background.

Line density should follow hierarchy. Equal emphasis on every strand, fold,
prop, and background edge can flatten the reading order even when each part is
individually detailed.

### Value, color, light, and rendering

Establish large value groups and the principal focal contrast before choosing
local colors. Then resolve palette relationships, warm-cool structure,
saturation hierarchy, light color, shadow color, and the degree to which the
environment affects character color.

Choose a coherent rendering language: flat color, one or more cel-shadow bands,
soft gradients, painterly modeling, textured brushwork, graphic halftone,
specular accents, rim light, bloom, particles, or other effects. Select only
effects that reinforce the intended artifact and hierarchy. Mixing hard cel
shadows, physically soft skin rendering, dense painterly texture, and unrelated
glow treatments without a controlling idea can make the style incoherent.

Describe how materials differ within that language. Hair, eyes, skin, metal,
glass, leather, and cloth need not be physically realistic, but they should not
all use the same highlights, edges, and value behavior unless flat graphic
treatment is intentional.

## Integrate character and environment

The background is part of the illustration's visual language. Coordinate
perspective, horizon or viewer height, light direction, palette, atmosphere,
line treatment, edge hierarchy, and detail density across the whole frame.
Use foreground overlap, contact shadows, cast shadows, reflected color, or
atmospheric depth when they help the character inhabit the space.

Decide whether the environment is narrative, iconic, decorative, or minimal.
A detailed story location should reveal something visible about the event; a
graphic or abstract background should still reinforce silhouette, movement,
palette, and hierarchy. Do not default to a generic blurred background merely
because the character is the subject.

For multiple people, use distinct silhouettes, controlled overlaps, local color
separation, gaze, and pose direction to preserve both identity and relationship.
Determine whether one character leads, the ensemble reads as a unit, or the
interaction itself is the focal subject.

## Research the requested visual language

Research when the request depends on a named subgenre, production period,
studio convention, title, illustrator, manga tradition, character design,
costume tradition, architecture, or symbolic motif that is not already
understood. A name is a research route, not a complete style instruction.

Analyze references through observable properties:

- proportion, facial abstraction, and silhouette;
- contour quality, weight, closure, and line color;
- value grouping, palette, saturation, and light-shadow structure;
- edge treatment, texture, rendering density, and effects;
- composition, camera relationship, depth, and background behavior; and
- the relationship among character design, setting, and visible story.

Use creator-owned illustrations, official model sheets, production art, layout
or animation materials, and direct examples of the relevant form where
available. Do not copy unrelated subject matter from a style reference. If the
request depends on anatomy, strong foreshortening, combat, dance, sport,
physical contact, costume construction, or an exact setting, research those
facts separately rather than expecting a style reference to answer them.

The medium design is ready when the intended artifact, abstraction,
proportions, line system, value and color structure, rendering, effects, and
background relationship form one compatible visual language and every named
reference has been translated into the properties that matter for this image.
