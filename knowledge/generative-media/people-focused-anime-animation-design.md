# People-focused anime-animation design

## Scope

This document explains how to research and evaluate the anime- or
manga-influenced animation treatment of a continuous shot centered on one or
more people. It owns medium-specific decisions about character layout, key
poses, timing and spacing, held, stepped, or fluid cadence, exaggeration,
animation-specific interaction, line and color continuity, layered backgrounds,
and two-dimensional parallax through time.

## When to update

Update this document when durable evidence from anime production, character
animation, temporal drawing systems, or generated animation changes the use of
poses, timing, cadence, exaggeration, line, color, layers, or planar motion
within this medium.

## Translate the shot into animation decisions

Anime animation is not a still illustration with motion added to every visible
element. Use the established shot purpose and performance to decide which
poses, temporal accents, held images, deformations, effects, layers, and camera
relationships should carry its meaning.

Treat layout as the bridge between the intended shot and its animation system.
Establish character scale and placement, background composition, movement
paths, important silhouettes, effects areas, layer relationships, and the
finished framing needed to realize the performance. Fine motion cannot rescue
a layout that hides the decisive pose or contact.

Stillness and held images are valid animation choices. A shot with selective
eye, mouth, hair, effect, layer, or camera movement can feel more intentional
than one in which every element drifts merely to prove that it is animated.

## Build the performance from key poses

Design the smallest useful set of significant poses before secondary motion:

- the visible initial pose supplied by the first frame;
- a preparatory or anticipation pose when the action needs one;
- the principal action, contact, or emotional pose;
- a reaction, overshoot, or consequence pose when it changes the reading; and
- the ending pose or continuing trajectory at the duration boundary.

Evaluate each important pose for silhouette, line of action, balance, weight,
torso and pelvis relationship, head direction, gaze, hands, contact, and
negative space. Stylized anatomy, foreshortening, and deformation can increase
force or clarity, but accidental shape drift is not expressive exaggeration.

Translate thought and feeling into coordinated eyes, brows, mouth, head,
shoulders, hands, posture, and breath. Decide whether a response develops
gradually or lands as a graphic accent, and ensure its facial or bodily change
remains visible at the selected scale and through any overlap.

Avoid unintended twinning: equal poses, mirrored limbs, and simultaneous
timing across the body can make an action mechanical. Asymmetry, sequential
adjustment, counteraction, and selective stillness can create clearer force and
personality.

## Separate timing, spacing, and cadence

Timing determines how long an action or pose occupies. Spacing determines how
far the presented positions change across that time. Close spacing creates
slower apparent movement, wider spacing creates faster movement, and changes
in spacing create acceleration, impact, settling, or hesitation.

Give important poses enough time to read. Decide where movement accelerates,
snaps, coasts, decelerates, stops, or holds; which changes occur together; and
which reactions need their own beat. Uniform spacing and continuous smoothing
can remove the intended impact even when the path is correct.

Choose held, stepped, and fluid motion by function rather than treating drawing
density as a quality scale:

- held poses with selective living movement can focus attention;
- stepped keys and large spacing can create force, shock, graphic humor, or
  stylized speed;
- fluid transitions can clarify mechanics, subtle acting, graceful motion,
  turns in depth, or sustained camera-relative movement; and
- cycles can support breathing, walking, crowds, weather, machinery, or
  decorative rhythm when repetition is intentional.

A shot may move between stillness, stepped accents, and fluid passages, and
different layers may use different cadences. Do not impose one universal
exposure rate or numerical rhythm. Describe the visible cadence unless exact
frame treatment is essential to the artifact and meaningful to its consumer.

A readable ending pose need not become a dead freeze. It may overshoot and
settle, retain subtle eye or breath behavior, or allow hair, cloth, effects, and
background motion to resolve after the principal pose arrives.

## Build a causal hierarchy of animated motion

Use anticipation to prepare attention or reveal intention. Let dependent parts
follow, overlap, overshoot, and settle according to the controlling action.
Supporting movement needs a cause and a subordinate role:

- gaze may lead a head turn, followed by shoulders and torso;
- balance and weight may shift before locomotion;
- hair, sleeves, fabric, accessories, and carried objects respond to
  acceleration, air, gravity, and contact;
- dust, water, debris, light effects, or environmental reaction follows its
  initiating event; and
- trailing elements may continue after the main body stops.

Choose the target and duration of exaggeration. A forceful body curve,
foreshortened limb, off-model impact drawing, facial take, speed effect,
smear-like transition, or camera accent can clarify force or emotion. Applying
equal exaggeration to character, camera, effects, and background removes
hierarchy and makes intentional deformation indistinguishable from failure.

For multi-person contact, express approach, contact, pressure or weight
response, release, and aftermath through readable poses and reaction timing.
Preserve each character's silhouette and design through crossing or overlap;
contacts that slide, swap, merge, or pass through bodies without a chosen
graphic convention are animation failures rather than general motion style.

## Preserve the animation system through time

Review continuity as several related systems:

- **Character model:** identity, proportions, facial structure, hair masses,
  costume construction, accessories, and relative scale.
- **Drawing system:** contour weight and color, internal-line hierarchy, shape
  language, curve behavior, and intentional texture or line boil.
- **Paint system:** local colors, cel-shadow grouping, highlight logic,
  material distinctions, and palette relationships.
- **Performance drawing:** deliberate deformation and off-model accents that
  return coherently to the established design.

Identity and shape must survive turns, foreshortening, occlusion, expression,
and contact. Lines should move with forms instead of crawling independently or
changing topology without cause. Stable color regions should not swap, pulse,
or bleed, while shadows and effects should evolve according to the intended
light or graphic system.

Intentional roughness, line boil, deformation, discontinuity, or color
fluctuation can be valid. Its amplitude, timing, and communicative purpose must
remain controlled rather than varying accidentally from frame to frame.

## Coordinate layers and two-dimensional parallax

Separate only the visual layers that matter: foreground occluders or effects,
principal characters and props, middle-ground action, background planes, sky or
distant fields, and graphic overlays. Decide which layers are held, internally
animated, translated, scaled, or revealed.

Use differences in layer movement to create physical depth or an intentionally
graphic multiplanar effect. Near and distant planes need not imitate one fully
three-dimensional camera, but their speeds, directions, scale changes, and
reveals should express a deliberate spatial system rather than making the
background swim as one sheet.

For a camera or layer move, preserve the selected character scale and placement,
relative foreground-background movement, important silhouettes, and designed
image area. Fixed framing remains an active animation choice. Constant
recentering, uniform layer speeds, or camera motion that smooths away decisive
poses weakens the medium treatment.

## Research the requested animation language

Research when a high-impact decision depends on unfamiliar or exact:

- a named anime, studio, animator, period, subgenre, or production form;
- an acting convention, expression, gaze pattern, gesture, or pantomime;
- dance, sport, martial art, ritual, craft, occupation, or specialized motion;
- multi-person contact, prop transfer, force, resistance, or reaction;
- costume, hair, articulated prop, vehicle, creature, or effects behavior;
- limited, stepped, fluid, cyclic, or deliberately irregular cadence; or
- a camera, layout, compositing, perspective, or layer convention.

Translate a named work or creator into observable pose design, cadence,
deformation, effects, line, color, and background treatment rather than using
the name as a substitute for animation direction. Analyze motion references at
normal speed and frame by frame when necessary, then express the relevant
poses, timing, paths, and contacts without leaving the reference video as an
undeclared dependency.

## Critique the animation treatment

Review whether significant poses form a readable performance; timing, spacing,
holds, and cadence express the intended force or emotion; anticipation,
follow-through, effects, and background response form a causal hierarchy; and
character model, lines, colors, light, layers, and parallax remain coherent.

Revise treatment that replaces acting with labels, smooths away decisive poses,
confuses held motion with a stalled image, moves every layer independently,
makes contact unreadable, or treats accidental identity and line drift as
expressive animation. Deliberate limited motion, graphic discontinuity,
off-model accents, and impossible planar space can pass when they remain
controlled and communicative.

The animation treatment is ready when its key poses, acting, timing, cadence,
supporting motion, drawing and paint systems, layers, parallax, and controlled
exaggeration form one coherent medium language throughout the shot.
