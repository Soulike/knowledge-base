# Maintained document quality

## Scope

This document defines the durable quality model for maintained factual,
explanatory, and instructional documents whose value depends on reliable
information, understanding, retrieval, or correct action. It covers why a
document should exist, what it may authoritatively own, how its form should fit
its readers' use, and how to keep and validate it through its useful life. It
does not define the artistic or rhetorical quality of creative writing or pure
persuasion.

## When to update

Update this document when evidence changes the durable relationship among
reader needs, document purpose, authority and evidence, information
architecture, maintenance lifecycle, or reader validation within this scope.

## Justify the maintained document

A maintained document is justified by a recurring reader and use, information
the reader cannot recover reliably from existing sources, and a meaningful
mistake, misunderstanding, or blocked action the document can prevent. Define
the reader by their goal, prior knowledge, context, and constraints rather than
by a role label alone. State or make evident what the document covers and which
reader outcome it supports.

Prefer no new document when a current authoritative source already satisfies
the need. Otherwise choose the lightest maintained form that makes the needed
information reliable and usable. More coverage is not automatically better;
include material because it supports the reader's use, not merely because it is
available.

Document form follows purpose. A tutorial supports learning through a guided
experience, a how-to guide supports completion of a known goal, reference
supports authoritative lookup, and explanation supports understanding concepts
and relationships. These responsibilities may coexist in a larger publication,
but each section should retain a clear reader purpose rather than blending every
form into one undifferentiated account.

## Establish authority and evidence

Every material fact, rule, decision, or explanation needs an appropriate
authority: direct observation, a controlled source, primary evidence, an
applicable rule, or qualified subject judgment. Determine which source should
change when the information changes. Let the document state a claim only when
it owns that claim; otherwise link readers to the authority instead of creating
a hand-maintained copy.

A generated view can project facts from another source when generation is part
of the same update path. It remains evidence of that source at generation time,
not independent authority for missing or editorially unsuitable information. A
source index similarly owns question-to-source routing while the linked sources
own the answers.

Authoring evidence and reader-facing content have different responsibilities.
Research, source collection, review evidence, rejected alternatives, drafts,
and task history may justify what an author writes without belonging in the
maintained document. Keep them in the work record that owns the authoring event
unless the intended reader needs them to find, understand, judge, maintain, or
act on the document's content. When traceability is itself part of the
document's responsibility, include only the provenance needed for that use
rather than reproducing the research process.

Distinguish established facts and requirements from interpretation, examples,
uncertainty, and preference. When readers must evaluate an analytical result,
expose the material evidence, assumptions, method, limitations, and uncertainty
needed to judge it. Scale subject-matter and evidence review to the information's
importance, volatility, and consequences.

Keep one-time plans, rollout status, investigations, and review evidence in the
record that owns that event. Move only durable conclusions or constraints into
maintained documentation.

## Design for reader use

Put the document's purpose or main message where readers can recognize it
early. Group related information, give headings and sections distinct jobs,
order prerequisites before dependent explanations or actions, and make needed
answers discoverable without requiring a complete linear read.

Use concrete subjects and actions, familiar words, and consistent terms. Define
specialized vocabulary before relying on it. Use lists, tables, diagrams, and
other structures only when they make the information easier to understand or
apply; no format is a quality requirement by itself.

Use an example when it resolves a real comprehension or application problem.
Make it accurate, representative, small enough to understand, and explicit
about the choice or result it demonstrates. State the broader rule so the
example does not silently narrow the supported cases or become an accidental
requirement.

## Maintain and validate the result

Give maintained information an owner or update path, observable change
triggers, applicable versions or conditions, and a way to revise, consolidate,
redirect, archive, or retire it. Describe one coherent current account. Keep
historical status in records meant to preserve history rather than layering old
and new behavior into the current explanation.

Validate the quality claim with evidence capable of supporting it. Author and
peer review can find many low-risk defects, but they do not by themselves prove
that intended readers can find, understand, or use the information. Depending
on risk, useful evidence may include a fresh reader's paraphrase, a realistic
find-and-use task, execution of an example, or observation of task completion.
Use neutral tasks and avoid coaching the expected answer.

Choose validation in proportion to the document's claims, frequency of use,
rate of change, and cost of misleading a reader. When the needed evidence is
unavailable, disclose the validation boundary instead of presenting author
confidence as demonstrated reader success.
