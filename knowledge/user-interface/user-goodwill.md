# User goodwill in interface design

## Scope

This document explains how an interface or service flow can preserve users'
willingness to continue by respecting their time, information needs, and
interests. It uses goodwill as a qualitative way to examine the burden a
product places on people, especially when they need an answer, must make a
commitment, or encounter a limitation.

## When to update

Update when evidence changes how avoidable work, withheld information,
unnecessary data requests, support, or recovery affect users' willingness to
continue and their assessment of whether a product serves their interests.

## Treat goodwill as a design question

People arrive with different levels of patience and trust. Their circumstances
matter too: someone dealing with an urgent disruption may have little tolerance
for promotional content or a long search for help. A clear interface can still
feel inconsiderate if it makes a person work to obtain information the product
could readily provide.

Think of goodwill as a limited, variable reserve that an experience can draw
down or replenish. This is a metaphor for reviewing a task, not a score to
assign to a person or a prediction of abandonment. A single consequential
obstacle can outweigh several minor conveniences. Nor does completing a task
prove that the product treated the user well; some people have no practical
alternative.

For each important task, ask what the person needs now, what the product knows,
and where the product is transferring its own cost or uncertainty to them.
Examine the whole path rather than judging each screen in isolation.

## Be candid before asking for commitment

Show information that could change a user's decision while they can still use
it: the total or material costs, availability, service disruptions, conditions,
and ways to get help. Make answers to pressing questions findable where people
are likely to look. If the situation is unresolved, state what is known, what
is uncertain, and when or how the user can learn more. Silence or routine
promotion during a known disruption can suggest that the product is ignoring
the user's problem.

Do not make people invest effort in a flow before revealing an unwelcome term
that was already known. A product may have legitimate constraints or business
tradeoffs, but those should be deliberate decisions whose burden is understood,
not accidental consequences of a page layout or a conversion goal. Candor does
not require overwhelming every screen with detail; it requires placing
decision-relevant facts before the decision they affect.

## Carry work on the product's side where possible

Make the main user goals straightforward to find and complete. Remove steps
that only compensate for internal constraints: accept reasonable input formats
when they mean the same thing, avoid collecting personal information that the
task does not need, and pass known context forward rather than making people
reenter it. A useful shortcut can be as small as a direct link that opens the
relevant result instead of handing the user an identifier to copy and search.

Conveniences should fit the task. A readable print or export view may matter
for content people need to keep; it is not a universal feature requirement.
Likewise, fewer steps are helpful when they remove needless work, but should
not conceal a material consequence or remove a necessary chance to correct an
error.

## Help people when the ideal path fails

Support content should answer questions people actually ask, including
uncomfortable ones, and be accurate and current. Do not use a question and
answer format as cover for promotion. When errors cannot be prevented, make
the recovery path apparent and preserve work where possible. When the product
cannot meet a reasonable request, acknowledge the inconvenience plainly and
explain any available alternative or next step. Even when there is no
workaround, acknowledge the burden rather than masking it with a generic claim
of care.

Presentation also communicates effort. A coherent, maintained interface can
help people trust that the service works; clutter, stale information, and
promotions that obstruct urgent tasks can suggest the opposite. Judge these
effects by whether people can address their task, rather than by isolated
opinions about colors or decorative taste.

## Check the burden in a real task

Walk through a representative task from the user's starting situation. Mark
where the user must search for an answer, disclose data, repeat work, accept an
unknown consequence, recover from an error, or seek help. Include time pressure
or a service problem when those are realistic conditions. Observe where people
hesitate, backtrack, give up, or express concern, and investigate the interface
or service condition behind the observation.

Use those observations to identify a concrete burden and its consequence. Do
not infer a numeric goodwill balance, a population failure rate, or a user's
inner motive from the metaphor or a small set of task observations.
