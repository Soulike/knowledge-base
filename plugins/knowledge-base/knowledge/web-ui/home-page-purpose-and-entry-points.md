# Home page purpose and entry points

## Scope

This document explains how a website home page helps a new or returning
visitor understand what the site offers, why it may be useful, and where to
begin, while balancing that job with featured content and other demands on
the page.

## When to update

Update when evidence changes how visitors understand a site's purpose and
offerings from its home page, find useful starting routes, return there after
entering elsewhere, or respond to competing home page content and promotions.

## Give the whole site a clear first reading

A visitor should be able to form a correct initial account of whose site this
is, what it offers, what they can do there, and why it may be worth their
attention. Make that account visible through the site's identity, a concise
description when needed, the main areas and features, and representative
content. Show enough of the range to let people recognize their interests
without listing every item. If the first impression suggests the wrong kind
of site, later details may be interpreted through that mistaken assumption.

The home page is not everyone's first page. A person may arrive at an article,
product, or task page and then visit home to understand who publishes the site,
whether it seems credible, or what else it contains. Design the home page to
answer those questions for someone who has not followed a path from it. Make
the publisher and the nature of its offerings clear enough to support that
judgment; a claim of trustworthiness alone cannot supply the evidence.

## Explain the proposition in visitor language

Use a short, prominent explanation when the site's purpose is not already
obvious from its identity, navigation, and content. A tagline, introductory
sentence, or clearly labeled explanation can do this; none is mandatory. When
using a tagline, connect it visually to the site's identity so visitors read
it as an explanation of the whole site.
Describe what the site or service actually provides and, when relevant, the
benefit or difference a visitor can use to decide whether to continue.
An aspirational motto or broad claim of excellence does not answer those
questions by itself.

Give the explanation as much space as its unfamiliarity or complexity
requires, then stop once the visitor can form the right picture. A novel
service may need a short example or a route to a fuller explanation. A familiar
site may need only a few clear words. Do not assume a video, a fixed tagline
length, or a large welcome block is needed. Make the essential explanation
available in the page itself for people who will not open another medium.

Let featured examples, category names, and page copy reinforce the same
account. A concise claim that conflicts with the visible content creates
uncertainty rather than clarity. Test the actual composed page, because a
correct sentence can still be overlooked among stronger promotions.

## Make the first useful step apparent

After recognizing the site, a visitor should be able to locate an appropriate
starting route. Depending on what the site offers, this may be search,
browsing by section, a primary task, a representative item, registration, or
sign-in. Give frequently sought destinations a direct route when making people
work through the site's hierarchy would only delay them. Give these routes
labels and appearances that predict what happens next. If several visitor
groups need different starts, distinguish their paths without making every
path equally prominent.

Promoted content can help someone discover value they did not arrive looking
for. If ongoing maintenance affects whether visitors trust the site, give an
honest sign that it is cared for, even when frequent return visits are not the
goal. Where currency or repeat visits matter, show genuinely current content
that helps visitors judge the site. Give such material a purpose in the
visitor's understanding or task. Keep the routes people commonly need
recognizable even when the page also carries news, featured items, commercial
offers, or account controls.

## Protect the page from competing promotions

Home page space and attention are finite. Each additional promotion may help
one part of the site while making the whole page harder to understand and the
main entry routes harder to find. Decide what deserves prominence by the
visitor's likely questions and tasks, not by the number of groups requesting
space. Avoid several equally forceful messages that all appear to be the
primary next step.

When a useful promotion cannot fit without obscuring the site's explanation
or starting routes, place it where its audience is more likely to need it,
such as a relevant section page, or use a bounded shared slot. Reassess the
combined page whenever a new item is added; small additions can gradually
erase the purpose of the layout.

## Check comprehension and action with unfamiliar visitors

Show the home page to people who do not already know the organization or
product, without first explaining it. After a brief look, ask what they
believe the site is for, what it offers, why they might use it, and where they
would start for a realistic task. Compare their account with the intended
one, then observe what they actually choose and where that route leads.

Include a case that starts on an internal page and moves to home for broader
context. Check the supported screen sizes and access paths, since the first
view and available routes can differ. A glance test can reveal a wrong first
impression; following the route tests whether the apparent starting point
works.
